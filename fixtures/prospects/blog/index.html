What the estuary remembers

By Mara Okafor · 9 minute read

Every spring the river delivers a fresh layer of silt to the estuary, and every layer keeps a record of the year it arrived: the floods, the dry summers, the seasons when the upstream mill was running and when it was not.

Last April I spent a week with a team from the regional survey office pulling sediment cores from the mudflats at low tide, and I came home with a new respect for how patient the evidence can be.

Reading a sediment core

A core is a plastic tube driven straight down into the mud and pulled out with a satisfying, reluctant gasp. Split lengthways, it shows bands of grey, brown and almost black, each a few millimetres thick.

The darker bands usually mark wet years, when more organic material washed down from the hills. The lighter, sandier bands record storms that churned up the channel and laid down coarse grains in a single night.

The mill years

About forty centimetres down, the bands turn a strange rusty orange. The survey team recognised it at once: iron-rich runoff from the paper mill that operated upstream for most of the last century.

When the mill closed, the orange stops abruptly. Above it, the bands return to their older colours, as if the estuary had simply waited for the interruption to end.

What comes next

The survey office plans to repeat the coring every five years. The newest layers will show whether the restored salt marsh upstream is trapping more sediment, which would slowly raise the mudflats against rising seas.

It is a slow experiment by human standards. The estuary, as the cores make plain, has never been in a hurry.
